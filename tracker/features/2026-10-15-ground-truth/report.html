Ground truth: planting known bad data to test a screening method

We built a copy of a robot-training dataset in which 91 of 960 candidate recordings carry a known, recorded kind of damage (seven kinds, 13 recordings each), a separate answer key, a gate that keeps a method from seeing the key, and a scorer that counts how many damaged recordings a method finds. The measured catch: cheap one-line statistics already find every kind (the hardest, a timing shift, at a score of 0.848 where 0.5 is guessing and 1.0 is perfect), so a method has to beat those, kind by kind, not just beat guessing.

Date 2026-10-15 → 2026-10-17 Milestone "Ground truth" (spec §10, p. 20) Stories 6 · Acceptance criteria met 44/44 Checker rounds 3 Status verified locally harm to a trained robot: pending server

In plain words

To judge a method that claims to spot harmful training data, you first need data where you already know which pieces are harmful. We built that: a copy of a robot-training dataset in which 91 of 960 recorded demonstrations (about one in ten) were deliberately damaged in seven different ways, with a separate answer key that lists exactly which ones, and a gate that stops any method under test from peeking at the key. We also built the scorer that counts how many of the damaged demonstrations a method finds. The result that matters is that simple one-line statistics, with no learning at all, already find every kind of damage fairly well (the hardest, a two-second timing shift, scores 0.848 on a scale where 0.5 is guessing and 1.0 is perfect), so a clever method only counts as clever if it beats those simple statistics kind by kind, not if it merely beats guessing. What is still unknown is whether the damage actually makes a trained robot worse; that needs training the robot model and testing it on a powerful server, which has not been done, so this report does not claim it.

Unfamiliar word? Every technical term is defined where it first appears and again in the glossary just below. Precise file names, spec citations and commands are in the tables and in the "Technical detail" part after the explanations.

The one result that matters. Not one of the seven kinds of damage is out of reach of a cheap statistic. Six of the seven are found at a ranking score of 0.95 or higher (the score is called AUROC, defined in the glossary) by a one-line statistic that uses no answer key. The timing shift (time_shift) is the hardest, at 0.848. So a method's results must be compared kind by kind against the best simple statistic for that kind (the table in "The result"), not against chance. The owner accepted this as a known limitation (the owner's ruling A, explained under "Owner decisions"); it is not a defect left behind. This is the report's headline (acceptance criterion AC5.9).

Not shown here. Whether the damage actually makes a trained robot model worse, and whether removing the damaged recordings makes it better, needs training the robot model (SmolVLA, defined below) and testing it on a server. Those steps are written down as server runbook items GT-R1 to GT-R6 and none has been run. Nothing in this report claims their outcome.

Scoring a method
Method, screeningA method is any procedure that ranks episodes from most to least suspicious. Screening is that ranking step. The method is "under test": we ask whether it puts our damaged episodes near the top.
RecoveryHow many of the planted damaged episodes a method finds.

Recall@13Of the 13 damaged episodes of one kind, the share that land within the 13 highest-ranked episodes. 1.0 means all 13 are at the very top; random guessing gives about 0.015. It is the spec's "recovered 90% of the injected bad episodes" currency.
PrecisionOf the episodes a method flags, the share that really are damaged. When 13 are flagged and 13 are planted, precision@13 equals recall@13, so only recall@13 is reported. (Average precision, also computed by the standard library, is a related ranking score.)
Heuristic, H1 to H8A cheap rule that gives every episode one suspicion score with no training and no answer key. We built eight, as baselines. H1 length in steps. H4 smoothness (the average step-to-step change in the arm command; high means rough). H5 duplicate frame pairs (consecutive camera images that are byte-for-byte identical). H6 instruction mismatch (the instruction differs from the usual one for that task). H7 length compared with the task's normal length, tried both longest-first and shortest-first. H8 post-release share (how much of the episode comes after the final gripper release, compared with the task's normal).
Bands (a), (b), (c)Our reporting buckets for the best score any heuristic reaches on a kind of damage: (a) 0.95 or higher, (b) from 0.6 to 0.95, (c) below 0.6, meaning "not found by any heuristic". They are our own convention (inferred), not from the spec.

What was built, step by step

The idea comes from the spec: plant bad data whose location you know, then ask whether a method finds exactly those episodes. That turns "the method says X" into "the method recovered a stated share of the planted bad episodes". Concretely:

  1. Start clean. Take the original dataset (v0): 1000 demonstrations over 20 tasks. Set aside 40 as clean reference episodes (2 per task); the other 960 are candidates.
  2. Choose what to damage. Pick 91 candidates with a fixed random seed (so the choice can be repeated): 13 for each of seven kinds of damage, 4 or 5 per task, with no task given the same kind twice. That is 9.48% of the 960 candidates.
  3. Damage them and write a new dataset version. Each chosen episode is rewritten as described below. The result is the corrupted dataset (v3). The answer key is written beside it, not inside it.
  4. Guard the door. A method may read the corrupted dataset only through the method-facing loader, which checks that nothing reveals which episodes were edited.
  5. Set the bar. Eight cheap rules (the heuristics H1 to H8) score every candidate with no answer key. Their results are the bar a real method has to clear.
  6. Score. A scorer takes any method's ranking and the answer key and reports AUROC and recall@13 for each kind of damage.

The seven kinds of damage

Each is one small function. They follow the spec's list of realistic data problems (§2.5, p. 5; §8.10, p. 17) and RoboDrop's settings (RoboDrop is one of the papers the spec builds on).

The bar to beat, kind by kind

For each kind of damage, the best that any of the eight simple rules (H1 to H8) achieves, measured on the corrupted dataset (13 damaged against 869 clean episodes per kind). Both numbers matter. AUROC says damaged episodes are generally ranked above clean ones. Recall@13 says how many land in the very top 13, which is the spec's "recovered 90%" currency. Why gripper_release scores AUROC 0.964 but recall@13 of 0.000

The two numbers answer different questions. AUROC is the chance that a random damaged episode outranks a random clean one, so it rewards being ranked "high". Recall@13 asks whether the 13 damaged episodes sit inside the top 13 of 882. Under the best rule for this kind (H8, the share of the episode after the final gripper release), the 13 damaged episodes rank 16, 17, 20, 23, 26, 27, 28, 29, 39, 48, 50, 78 and 97 (measured). All are in the top 100, but 15 clean episodes, whose hands naturally stay open for a long tail (task-relative shares of 0.24 to 0.57, analyzer-measured), sit above the best damaged one. So the rule finds this kind in the AUROC sense and misses it in the recall sense. So what: a future method should be judged on both numbers, and the spec's headline claim, "recovered 90% of the injected bad episodes", is a recall statement.

Eight simple rules against seven kinds

How the timing-shift fix changed the result

After round 1 the checker found that our first version of time_shift (in v2) padded the end of the episode with 40 idle steps, a giveaway that clean data never has (clean episodes have at most 21 idle steps in a row). The owner decided to remove it. The corrected version (in the corrupted dataset, v3) simply trims 40 steps instead.

Where the 91 sit

What is still unknown

Whether the damage actually hurts a trained robot is pending server: it needs training the robot model on the corrupted dataset and testing it with many rollouts, which this Mac cannot do at scale. The server runbook (artifacts/server-runbook.md) lists the steps in order, none run:

ItemWhat it would do
GT-R1Train SmolVLA on the corrupted candidates (v3/candidate), using the same training settings as the earlier evaluation feature (its AC8.5 configuration).
GT-R2Test each trained model with rollouts, using the earlier feature's test protocol (AC5.8) and a batch plan equal to the clean arm's.
GT-R3Turn the rollouts into returns (the per-metric numbers the evaluation feature computes).
GT-R4The verdict: corrupted-data policy (v3/candidate) against clean-data policy (v0/candidate), first the significance test (the macro-KS gate) and then per-metric intervals. It is a finding, not an acceptance condition.
GT-R5Optional: the oracle arm, 869 episodes with the 91 damaged ones removed.
GT-R6The hand-off: score a later method per kind against the bar-to-beat table.

LIBERO has no ghost-event rule (the spec's way of scoring a dropped item as a hard failure), so the hypothesis that a gripper release causes "drops" can only show up as lower success, a lower share of attempts finishing by a given time (F(τ)), or more timeouts. The estimated total disk for the runbook is 50 to 95 GB plus headroom (inferred). Also not covered: per-kind harm (it needs seven training arms), running RoboDrop, DemInf or CUPID (three published data-quality or attribution methods) against this ground truth, per-metric attribution, scoring pieces of an episode rather than whole episodes, and damage on the real-robot set the spec also mentions.

How we know it is right

An independent checker (the "analyzer") re-ran every verification script itself, into a scratch directory, once per round, three rounds. The details are in "Technical detail" below. In short: the stored data matches the answer key byte for byte (1000 of 1000 fingerprints), the scorer matches hand-computed answers, and the headline numbers were recomputed independently with a standard statistics library. Unverified or only read, said plainly: the story-6 code was not re-run in round 3 (it passed in round 2 and was not touched); the server runbook's commands and its disk and time figures have never been run on a server; the detour reversal (negated arm commands on the backward leg) was not replayed in the simulator; and whether the extra disk space seen on a freshly written copy is later reclaimed is inferred.

Disputes and owner rulings

Round 1, the detectability dispute (dispute-r1.md). The question put to the owner: "Ground truth dispute: 5 of 7 corruption types are found by one-line statistics. Which detectability leaks should be removed in the single re-derivation?" The analyzer recommended Option A. Ruling A, verbatim (2026-10-17): "A. Fix time_shift only (rec.)". That means:

  • The time_shift idle tail became a trim: observations 0 … T−41 paired with actions 40 … T−1, the length change (−40) recorded in the label. Measured effect: idle_steps AUROC on time_shift went from 1.000 to 0.445.
  • Everything else was kept, and recovery is reported per type against the heuristic baselines, saying plainly which types test anything beyond one-line statistics.
  • B (hiding provenance, correlated noise) and C (re-rendered idle) were not built; D arose later (below).

The ruling was final and the analyzer did not re-open it. Cost: one more full-scale derivation (v3, 562 s, free disk 86 to 78 GiB).

  • v3: 7,326,736,215 bytes = 7.33 GB = 6.82 GiB (byte sum, re-computed for this report, artifacts/summary_sizes.txt). v2: 7,346,100,191 bytes = 6.84 GiB. du -sh is not used because on a freshly written copy it printed 7.4G and kept falling.
  • For the owner: v2's physical copy, data/lerobot/libero_spatial_10__v2 (about 6.8 GiB), is still on disk. No agent deleted it. Deleting it is your call; it can be re-derived in about 9 minutes.

Known issue for the attribution feature. vlapoc.runs.train_run and eval_run (the functions that train and test a policy) bypass open_for_method and the blindness check. That is right for this feature's own training arms, but a later method that trains policies (datamodels, CUPID) could pass a ground-truth-derived subset to train_run and not be refused, and train_run("v1/candidate", …) would run on the fenced v1. The attribution feature must route its training inputs through the loader.

How to read the code

The pipeline: choose episodes, corrupt them while writing a new dataset version, let a method screen it without seeing the answer, then score its ranking against the answer key.

  1. tracker/features/2026-10-15-ground-truth/brief.md, "Owner decisions" and "Owner rulings": clipping, scope, blindness now, and ruling A.
  2. vlapoc/corruptions.py: the seven types as pure functions. Read make_edit, then action_noise (simplest), time_shift (changes length, ruling A) and clip_actions (decision 1). Each type's params says whether each parameter is quoted, measured or inferred.
  3. vlapoc/derive.py, EpisodeEdit and derive_version: applies edits while writing the new dataset in the parent's order, writes labels.parquet and parents.parquet, and refuses to register a version that is not blind.
  4. vlapoc/lineage.py: compare_blind, check_blind, assert_blind (what counts as leaking an edit: the instruction is content, provenance is bookkeeping) and write_fence (v1 fenced for a positional leak, v2 as superseded).
  5. vlapoc/groundtruth.py: select_injection, build_edits and derive_injection (the v3 command), then recovery_metrics, score_recovery, leak_check, and last retire_version.
  6. vlapoc/method_data.py: open_for_method, the one door a method uses.
  7. vlapoc/methods/heuristics.py: H1–H8 through that door; scan_episodes and compute are the core. Their per-type scores are the table to beat.
  8. The verifiers in story order, each docstring holding its run command: scripts/verify_corruptions_s1.py, verify_derive_gt_s2.py, verify_method_boundary_s3.py, verify_injection_s4.py, verify_recovery_s5.py with s5_recovery_table.py, and verify_consumers_s6.py.
  9. tracker/features/2026-10-15-ground-truth/artifacts/server-runbook.md: what still needs the server.
